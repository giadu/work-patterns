# Substack Draft Starter

## Overview

Turn rough observations from real work into a clean first draft for Substack. Focus on extracting one clear idea, choosing a strong angle, preserving the author's voice, and avoiding NDA or over-sharing mistakes.

## Workflow

### 1. Capture the raw material

Ask for the minimum useful inputs if they are missing:

- what happened, changed, or was noticed;
- why it mattered;
- who the post is for; and
- anything that must stay private.

If the author is unsure about the angle, read [article-modes.md](references/article-modes.md) and propose three directions.

### 2. Distill the idea before drafting

Identify:

- the core thesis in one sentence;
- why a reader should care;
- what is specific or unusual about the case; and
- what must be generalized because it is internal, sensitive, or too company-specific.

Prefer concrete observations over generic advice.

### 3. Pick one post shape

Choose one shape and stay with it:

- technical lesson;
- workflow or process insight;
- AI in practice;
- systems note;
- postmortem or what changed my mind; or
- why this exists.

### 4. Produce a useful writing package

Unless the author asks for only one artifact, generate:

- three title options;
- one recommended angle;
- a short thesis statement;
- a tight outline;
- a rough Substack draft;
- a short sensitivity check; and
- an optional short note or social summary.

### 5. Save only with an agreed location

If the author asks for a file, save a Markdown draft in the agreed writing directory. Do not assume a machine-specific output path. Use a clean kebab-case filename, avoid overwriting existing drafts, and return the final path.

## Writing rules

- Default to English unless another language is requested.
- Write in the author's voice, not generic AI blog voice.
- Prefer clarity over hype and concrete observations over abstract advice.
- Avoid filler and performative certainty.
- Keep the first draft editable rather than over-polished.
- Generalize live-work details unless the author explicitly confirms they are safe to publish.

## Sensitivity check

Before finalizing, remove or generalize:

- confidential architecture or implementation detail;
- customer names, internal metrics, proprietary prompts, thresholds, or incidents;
- internal identifiers and roadmap information; and
- claims the author could not comfortably defend in public.
